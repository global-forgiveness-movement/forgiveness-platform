Does this actually work?

It’s a fair question, and one we have spent years working on. Below is what the research shows, what it does not yet show, and where you can read it for yourself.

The headline study

International REACH forgiveness intervention: a multisite randomised controlled trial

Ho, Worthington, Cowden, Bechara et al. · BMJ Public Health, 2024 · Trial NCT04257773

The largest randomised controlled trial of an interpersonal forgiveness intervention conducted to date. Between February 2020 and September 2021, over 4,500 participants took part across 5 relatively high-conflict countries — Colombia, Hong Kong, Indonesia, South Africa, and Ukraine.

Participants worked through the self-directed REACH workbook. A waitlist group received the same materials after a two-week delay. At two weeks, the immediate group showed significantly lower unforgiveness, depression, and anxiety.

What it does not show: the effects are definitively established only at a two-week follow-up. Improvements in depression and anxiety continued to four weeks, but because the waitlist group eventually received the same materials, the trial could not test how long the benefit lasts.

How much forgiveness work is enough?

A useful question for anyone planning a group or a curriculum. Across the wider forgiveness literature, benefit broadly scales with time invested. But the international trial found something encouraging: on forgiveness of the target hurt, the 2–3 hour self-directed workbook performed comparably to a six-hour group program.
